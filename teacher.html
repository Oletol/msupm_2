Warm-up and Speaking

Some numbers to argue with, the vocabulary of land and the people who administer it, and four small tools for thinking about who gets which hectare.

Land is the oldest subject of public administration and still the most difficult. It cannot be created, moved or hidden, everybody needs some of it, and almost everywhere the people who use it and the people who are registered as owning it are not the same people. This section gives you the words for that problem.

TeacherThe Warm-up at a glance

The section now has four parts. Lead-in (1.1–1.2) activates opinions and world knowledge before any vocabulary is taught. Building the vocabulary (1.3–1.8) presents the 16 core terms and then makes students handle them: in context, in contrast, by category and by register. Thinking it through (1.9–1.12) gives four open-ended tools with no key; their purpose is to produce disagreement worth talking about. From words to speaking (1.13–1.14) turns the language into a timed FCE-style long turn.

Procedure

What to listen for
  • Q1: students may name the university, the city or the state. In Russia the answer is in the Unified State Register of Real Estate (ЕГРН); an extract (выписка) settles it. This is a useful bridge to cadastre and title deed in 1.3.

Discussion: what to listen for

Possible confusions
  • tenure is the relationship, a title deed is the paper that proves one kind of it.
  • compensation pays for land taken; restitution gives the land itself back.
  • encroachment is gradual and by neighbours; a land grab is large-scale and usually involves the state or an investor.

Discussion: what to listen for

Thinking it through

The four tasks below have no answer key. Each one produces a result – a number, a profile, a plan, a chart – and the result is only the starting point for the discussion that follows it.

TeacherRunning the four open tasks

These tasks have no key by design. What you assess is the quality of the reasoning and the language used to express it. Three principles help:

  1. Make the result public. Ask students to show their screen to a partner, or project one result and ask the class to interpret it.
  2. Ask about the tool, not just the result. Each tool simplifies something; the best discussions are about what it leaves out.
  3. Collect one sentence in writing from every student before the discussion starts, so that quieter students have something ready to say.

The text

The Map and the Ground

Every land administration begins with an act of description. Somebody walks across a piece of ground, decides where it ends, writes that down and gives the description a number. Everything that follows – taxation, mortgage, inheritance, expropriation, compensation – depends on that first piece of writing being both accurate and accepted. The two are not the same thing, and the gap between them is where most land conflict lives.

For thirty years the standard remedy for insecure tenure has been registration. The argument is straightforward and, on its own terms, correct. A household with a title deed can prove what it holds, borrow against it, sell it at a fair price and defend it in court. A household without one is exposed to every stronger neighbour, every corrupt official and every investor with a map. Donors financed large registration programmes across three continents, and in a number of countries the number of registered parcels rose from almost nothing to a substantial proportion of the territory within a decade.

The results have been mixed in an instructive way. Where registration recorded what people were already doing, it worked. Where it recorded something else, it did harm. The commonest failure was the simplest: a survey team arrives, asks who the owner is, and writes down the name of the man who answers. The land may have been held by a household in which three women had independent rights of cultivation; it may have been grazed by pastoralists who were not present in October; it may have been managed by a village council that allocated plots for a season at a time. None of that fits in the box on the form. The register is then accurate about a situation that does not exist, and it is now the legally authoritative version.

This is why the distinction between customary and statutory systems matters so much in practice. Customary rights are usually layered: the same field may carry a right of cultivation, a right of passage, a dry-season grazing right and a right of the wider family to object to a sale. Statutory ownership is designed to be single and exclusive, because that is what makes a parcel easy to trade. Converting the first into the second does not simply record rights; it destroys most of them and concentrates what is left in one pair of hands. The people whose rights disappear rarely understand what has happened until somebody tries to sell.

None of this is an argument against registration, and the alternative that is sometimes proposed – leaving customary systems alone – has its own severe costs. Customary authorities can be arbitrary, they frequently exclude women and newcomers, and they are almost defenceless against a concession granted in the capital. The practical question is not whether to register but what to write down, and in what order. Several administrations now record use rights first, in a public process in which neighbours must confirm each claim, and convert to full ownership only later and only on request. The process is slower and much harder to photograph for a donor report, and it produces registers that people actually recognise.

The second lesson concerns what happens after the first entry. A register is not a monument; it is a claim about the present that decays every time somebody dies, marries, divides a plot between children or moves away. If the cost of recording a transaction exceeds what an ordinary household can pay in money and time, people will transact informally, and within fifteen years the register will describe a country that no longer exists. Maintenance is unglamorous, it is the first thing cut when budgets tighten, and it is the difference between a functioning system and an expensive archive.

The third lesson is about disputes. Every registration programme generates them, because writing a boundary down forces disagreements into the open that everybody had found it convenient to leave vague. That is not a defect. It is the purpose. But it means that a programme which produces forty thousand entries and no corresponding capacity to hear objections will simply overwhelm whatever tribunal exists, and the resulting backlog does more damage to public confidence than the original insecurity did. Dispute resolution is not something to be added afterwards if funds permit.

What emerges from the evidence is unfashionably modest. Land administration cannot settle the question of who ought to hold the land; that is a political question and it will be decided politically. What a competent land administration can do is make the existing situation legible, keep the record honest as it changes, and provide a forum in which a person who believes they have been wronged can say so and be heard within a reasonable time. Those three things are unspectacular, they are expensive to sustain, and no administration that has abandoned them has ever been forgiven quickly.

Grammar in Use

Tenses, the passive and modal verbs, delivered in four portions with nineteen exercises, all set in land administration.

Three structures carry almost all administrative writing about land. Tenses place a claim in time. The passive decides whether the actor is named. Modal verbs decide how strongly the state is speaking. Work through one portion at a time.

Portion A: tenses in a policy context

RulePresent Simple and Present Continuous

Present Simple for what is permanently or generally true: powers, duties, procedures, definitions. The council allocates plots at the beginning of each season.

Present Simple for repeated action, often with an adverb of frequency. Adverbs go before the main verb but after be: The register is rarely updated.

Present Continuous for what is in progress now or around now, and for a changing trend: The backlog is growing by about forty cases a month.

State verbs (know, belong, contain, depend, seem) are not normally used in the continuous.

RulePresent Perfect and Present Perfect Continuous

Portion B: the passive

RuleForm and use of the passive

Form: be in the required tense + past participle. The plots are allocated · were allocated · have been allocated · are being allocated · will be allocated · must be allocated.

Use the passive when the action matters more than the actor: Four hundred objections were lodged.

Use it when the actor is obvious, unknown or deliberately unnamed: The boundary was moved. Administrative writing uses this constantly, and part of reading it critically is asking who is missing.

Use it to keep the topic at the front of the sentence: The valley has been grazed by the same families for a century.

Add by + agent only when the agent carries information: The concession was granted by the Ministry of Industry, but not The form was signed by somebody.

RuleObligation, prohibition and permission

must – obligation the speaker imposes or accepts. have to – obligation from an outside rule. Every objection must be heard · Applicants have to attend in person.

RuleModals of deduction

About the present: must (I am almost certain it is true), may / might / could (it is possible), cannot (I am almost certain it is not true). The file must be in the archive · It cannot be the same parcel.

need not have + past participle means it was done but was unnecessary; did not need to usually means it was not done at all.

Writing

The paragraph, the topic sentence and the punctuation that holds an argument together.

Writing is built from the bottom up. Before anyone can write a convincing report or essay they have to write a convincing paragraph, and a paragraph is convincing when the reader knows, from the first line, what it is for.

How a piece of written work is put together

RuleThe three levels of a piece of writing

The whole text. An essay of this kind has an introduction that states the question and your position, two or three body paragraphs that each defend one idea, and a conclusion that answers the question without introducing anything new.

The paragraph. One paragraph carries one idea. If you cannot say in a single short sentence what a paragraph is about, it contains two ideas and should be divided.

The sentence. Sentences inside a paragraph are not independent. Each one either supports, illustrates, qualifies or contrasts with the idea announced at the top.

RuleThe topic sentence

The topic sentence states the idea of the paragraph. It is normally the first sentence, occasionally the second, and only rarely the last.

A good topic sentence makes a claim, not an announcement. Registration cannot record layered rights is a topic sentence; Now I will discuss registration is not.

It should be general enough to cover everything in the paragraph and specific enough to exclude what is not there.

Everything after it is support: an example, a figure, a reason, a concession, a contrast, a consequence.

Test: cover the paragraph and read the topic sentence alone. If a reader can predict what follows, it works.

Punctuation

RuleFive rules that cover most mistakes

Capital letters for the first word of a sentence, for names of people, places, institutions and documents (the Ministry of Land, the Land Registration Act), for nationalities and languages, and for months and days. Not for job titles in general (a registrar, a commissioner) unless attached to a name (Commissioner Petrov).

Commas in a list separate three or more items: The file contained a decision, a receipt and a photograph. Do not put a comma before the final and in a simple list.

A comma after a linking word or an opening subordinate clause: However, the fee was never reviewed. When the survey team arrived, the herders were at the summer pasture. If the subordinate clause comes second, no comma is needed.

Commas around extra information that could be removed: The registrar, who had nine years of service, refused to sign. If the information identifies which person you mean, use no commas: The official who signed the entry has retired.

Apostrophes show possession (the claimant's file, the holders' rights) or a missing letter (it's = it is). The possessive its has no apostrophe.

Task 5: reading, multiple choice

What a land office can actually promise

There is a temptation, in any ministry that deals with land, to promise justice. The temptation should be resisted, not because justice does not matter but because a land office is not the institution that delivers it. Who ought to hold the land in a country is a political question. It is answered by legislatures, by courts of general jurisdiction and, more often than anybody admits, by force. A district registrar has no part in that answer and should not pretend otherwise.

What a land office can promise is narrower and, precisely because it is narrower, deliverable. First, that the record will describe what is actually there. This sounds trivial and is not: it requires the office to write down rights that do not fit its own forms, and to resist the pressure to produce a clean register rather than a true one. Second, that the record will be kept current. A register is not a monument but a claim about the present, and a claim about the present has to be renewed. Third, that anybody who believes the record is wrong can say so, to a person with the power to change it, within a period they can survive.

None of these three promises is glamorous. None of them can be photographed for a donor report. All three are expensive to keep, and the expense is recurrent rather than capital, which makes it the first thing a finance ministry cuts. That is why so many countries possess an excellent register of the situation in 2012 and no reliable information about the situation today.

There is a fourth thing a land office can do, although it belongs more to conduct than to procedure. It can refuse to describe its own failures as the failures of the people it serves. When a household does not register a transfer, the honest question is what the office is charging, not whether the household is ignorant. When objections are not lodged, the honest question is where the notice was posted and in what language. An administration that habitually locates the problem in the citizen will never find it, because it is not there.
